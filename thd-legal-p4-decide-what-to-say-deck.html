Decide What You Want to Say

8 steps to update your outline · 9 slides · use Next or swipe

Step 1 of 8

Find your person

Make leaders care about one person before they see a number.

  • YouThe last time a store camera, app or self-checkout collected something about you, and whether anyone asked first.
  • Someone you knowA parent or friend who uses self-checkout and didn't know a camera might be scanning them. Ask them for five minutes.
  • An example characterBuilt from your Projects 1–3 research, like a shopper at an Illinois self-checkout with a camera.

Write 3 lines in your outline: who they are, the moment the problem hit, and what it cost them (time, money, trust or a project).

One extern opened with his grandfather, who walked away from a door install over a $40 fee. Leaders were still quoting it at the end.

Step 2 of 8

Keep it honest

A true, specific story beats a dramatic one.

  • Quote only what someone actually saidYou, a relative who agreed, or a public review or article. Name the source on the slide.
  • No made-up quotes for an example characterDescribe what happens to them instead, and label the slide: "Example based on [your sources]."
  • First names onlyFor anyone you know, and ask before you use their story.

Step 3 of 8

Write your title in one sentence

It's the one sentence on slide 1. Every other slide proves it. Pick the shape that fits.

The verdict
"[Do it / Don't / Not yet] for [where], because [the one reason]."
Problem → move
"[Who] [loses what] because [cause], so The Home Depot should [action]."
Start small
"The Home Depot should [action], starting with [the smallest first step]."

Example: Illinois requires written consent before any face scan, so The Home Depot should confirm which self-checkout cameras identify faces and get consent before they do.

A topic isn't a title. "Biometric privacy laws" names a subject. If there's no recommendation, keep going.

Step 4 of 8

Give the problem a name

Two to four words leaders can repeat in their next meeting. Write it under your title.

  • A picture wordThe communication black hole.
  • Two words that belong togetherLoyalty leakage.
  • Your person's own wordsPay-to-play.

Use the name the same way on your slides and in your talk.

In real presentations, one problem became "the Silence Project" the same day.

Step 5 of 8

Map your outline to five parts

Your outline's five headings match the five parts. Your deck tells the story in 8–10 slides.

Step 6 of 8

Sketch your slide list

Step 7 of 8

Add one piece of proof per slide

Next to each slide line, write what proves it and where it comes from (Projects 1–3 only).

At least one proof is a size: what the problem costs in dollars or hours. Leaders asked "what's it worth?" at both real final presentations.

  • The trade-off"We protect a $40 fee and risk a $2,000 installation."
  • Per person or per jobHours a week for one associate.
  • Your own resultThe damages per violation times a realistic number of shoppers, labeled as an estimate.

Small and sourced beats big and guessed. No proof? Say so: "The law says X. I'm assuming Home Depot does Y. I'd need the legal team to confirm Z."

Step 8 of 8

Take the 60-second test

Out loud, timed: your person, then one sentence each for parts 3, 4 and 5.

Example: Illinois requires written consent before collecting a face scan, and damages run $1,000 to $5,000 per violation. I'd lead with a DO fix, written consent before any camera that identifies a person, and SAY it to leadership now. First step: in 60 days, confirm which cameras identify faces, with a person checking every AI-flagged law update.

Stumbled? That slide isn't clear yet. Fix its line in your outline.

Then write one line you want the legal team to repeat. Try two sizes side by side, your person's words, or a plain promise. Use it once, near the end.

"Stepping over dollars to pick up dimes." · "Who's coming to my house should never be a mystery."

Done check

Your outline is ready when it has…

  • Your title in one sentence, plus the name of the problem
  • Your person: who, the moment, what it cost
  • Five headings with a list of 8–10 slides underneath
  • Proof for every slide, with its source or a "guess" label, and at least one size in dollars or hours
  • One line you want them to remember
  • A short appendix list: Limits & Open Questions, your state comparison map and your sources

Nothing to submit. Next you'll build the deck in Canva.
