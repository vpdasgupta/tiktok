The plan

How we turn three numbers into one date

We have an upfront cost of $2,400, a monthly savings of $200, and a monthly cost of $40. In three moves we'll combine them into a single answer: the month this aisle breaks even.

Move 1: net the monthly numbers.  Move 2: divide the upfront cost by that net.  Move 3: notice what the ongoing cost did to the answer.

2 / 4

Move 1 · Net it out

What the aisle really saves each month

The savings and the ongoing cost both repeat every month, so subtract one from the other.

Net monthly savings
Ongoing savings minus ongoing cost — what's actually left over each month.

$200 − $40 = $160 net per month

3 / 4

Move 2 · Divide

How long until the savings catch up

You start $2,400 in the hole. At $160 recovered each month, how many months to climb out?

Breakeven point (payback period)
Upfront cost ÷ net monthly savings — the month total savings finally equal what you spent.

$2,400 ÷ $160/mo = 15 months

4 / 4

Move 3 · The catch

Why that small $40 matters so much

Watch what the ongoing cost did to the date:

Without the $40 cost
Net is the full $200/month → $2,400 ÷ $200 = 12 months.
With the $40 cost
Net drops to $160/month → $2,400 ÷ $160 = 15 months.

A small monthly cost pushed breakeven three months later. In warehouse aisles — forklifts, ladders, pallet strikes — labels get damaged far more often, so that cost runs higher and the date slips further.
