How The Home Depot thinks — the four traits

Conservative

The Home Depot moves in careful, tested steps and tends to lean on what has worked before rather than betting big on the untried. A pitch that asks for a sweeping change reads as risky; one that starts small reads as smart.

  • Frame it as a pilot. Propose testing your idea in one store, one aisle, or one product category first, with a plan to expand only if it works.
  • Point to precedent. Note a time the idea (or a close cousin) already worked, so it feels proven rather than experimental.

Highly visual

Presentation carries weight here. Clean, on-brand slides and signage are the norm, so a cluttered slide can quietly undercut an otherwise strong idea.

  • Keep slides simple. One clear point per slide, plenty of white space, and a chart or image instead of a wall of text.
  • Show, don't just tell. A quick before/after visual or mockup of your recommendation lands faster than a paragraph describing it.

Formulaic

The Home Depot prefers repeatable methods and clear steps over improvising a new approach each time. That is good news when you are learning: you can follow the method instead of guessing.

  • Show your steps. Lay out how you got to your recommendation so someone else could repeat it and reach the same result.
  • Reuse the standard format. Match the structure and templates the team already uses rather than inventing your own.

Evidence-driven

Decisions here run on checked numbers and facts, not opinions or vendor claims. Any statement you make is only as strong as the source behind it.

  • Cite every claim. Attach a real number and where it came from to each point, and skip anything you can't back up.
  • Check the source. Favor The Home Depot's own data or a credible outside source over marketing copy, and note it so others can verify.
