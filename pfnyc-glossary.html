Group 1 of 5

Company Stage

How far along a company is — and how you read it at a glance.

  • Startup

    A young company built to grow fast, usually around a new product or technology.

    Why a scout cares: Almost everything you source is a startup — reading how far along it is, is half the job.

  • Beta

    A working early version of a product that real users can actually try, before it’s fully finished.

    Why a scout cares: ETL’s gate requires at least a working beta with active users — so “beta or better” is a pass/fail signal you check on every company.

  • Seed / Series A

    Seed is usually the first real outside investment; Series A is the next, larger round once a company is proving itself.

    Why a scout cares: Quick shorthand for how far along and how well-funded a startup is — a one-word read on stage.

Quick check

A startup’s site has a polished product page and a “Join the waitlist” button, but no sign anyone is using the product yet. How does that read against ETL’s “working beta with active users” bar?

Group 2 of 5

Signals to Weigh

The evidence that tells you a company is really working — and one plus to flag.

  • Active users

    Real people or organizations using the product right now — not just names on a waitlist.

    Why a scout cares: Proof the product works in the real world, not only on a slide. A waitlist is a promise; active users are traction.

  • Traction

    Evidence a product is catching on: real customers, deployments, usage, or revenue.

    Why a scout cares: Traction separates a company that’s shipping from one that’s still just a pitch — the difference between “could work” and “is working.”

  • M/WBE

    Minority- and Women-owned Business Enterprise — a company owned by people from those groups.

    Why a scout cares: ETL especially encourages M/WBE companies. Flag it as a plus, but never gate on it — a non-M/WBE company still qualifies.

Quick check

A strong, otherwise-eligible company is not an M/WBE. How should that affect your call?

Group 3 of 5

Ownership & Eligibility

Who owns whom — the fastest way to spot a company that can’t qualify.

  • Subsidiary

    A company that is owned and controlled by another, larger company (its parent).

    Why a scout cares: ETL takes independent companies only. A subsidiary of a public company fails the eligibility gate — spotting one saves you from rating a company that can’t qualify.

  • Parent company

    The larger company that owns one or more subsidiaries.

    Why a scout cares: If your find is a subsidiary — or is itself a parent with its own subsidiaries — check ownership before you trust the rest of your read.

  • Public vs. private

    A public company sells shares anyone can buy on a stock exchange and files its financials openly; a private company doesn’t.

    Why a scout cares: ETL takes private, independent companies. Public companies — and their subsidiaries — are out. One of the fastest disqualifiers to check.

Quick check

You find a promising water-sensor company, but it turns out to be a wholly-owned subsidiary of a large, publicly-traded utility. What’s the right first-pass call for ETL eligibility?

Group 4 of 5

Research Tools

The two databases you’ll reach for first on any new company.

  • Crunchbase

    A free/freemium online database of companies: funding, founders, locations, and ownership.

    Why a scout cares: A fast first stop to check a startup’s stage, money raised, and whether it’s independent — often your opening move on a new company.

  • SEC EDGAR

    The U.S. government’s free database of public-company filings (SEC = Securities and Exchange Commission).

    Why a scout cares: If a company or its parent files here, it’s public — an instant independence check. No filings is a signal it’s likely private.

Quick check

You want the fastest, most definitive free check of whether a company (or its parent) is publicly traded. Which source do you reach for?

Group 5 of 5

Working the Pipeline

Where your work lands — and the team hygiene that keeps it trustworthy.

  • Proof-of-concept (POC)

    A small, funded trial where a company shows its product can actually solve the real problem.

    Why a scout cares: The prize at the bottom of the funnel — the paid trial DEP runs with the winners you help surface. Everything you do points toward it.

  • Pipeline

    The running, shared list of companies the team is tracking — ideally one clean record per company.

    Why a scout cares: You work inside it alongside other scouts. Keeping it clean and claiming a company before you work it is basic team hygiene.

  • Deduplication

    Removing duplicate entries so each company shows up only once.

    Why a scout cares: Two scouts logging the same startup wastes effort. “Dedup” keeps the shared pipeline trustworthy — and keeps you from claiming a find someone already has.

Quick check

You spot a promising startup and you’re about to research and log it. Two teammates are scouting the same challenge. What’s the basic pipeline hygiene move?
