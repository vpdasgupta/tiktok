The big picture

Three things are graded, each worth part of your score

You're scored on how clearly you think and how well you explain your numbers. A fancy spreadsheet or one "right" answer won't earn points on its own.

  • 40% of your score

    Evidence Rigor

    Do you reach a real payback date, and can you show where every number came from?

  • 30% of your score

    THD Fit & Specificity

    Is your model built for The Home Depot (THD) and its warehouse-style stores, not a generic store?

  • 30% of your score

    AI Integration + Human Judgment

    Do you use AI to go faster, and show where you checked, fixed, or overruled it?

Showing your work is the grade. Two externs both get "about 12 months." One writes "source: my assumptions." The other lists three inputs, where each came from, and where they overruled the AI. The second scores much higher, with the same math.

Criterion 1 of 3

Evidence Rigor

40% of your score

Can you reach a real payback date, and trace every number to a source?

✓ Exceeds ★★★★★

  • You reach a real payback number: how many months until electronic shelf labels (ESL) pay for themselves.
  • You carry the labor savings you found in Pre-Work into the model as the savings side.
  • Every number has a source: a fact Home Depot's store team (MET, the merchandising execution team) gave you, a Home Depot number, a benchmark you cite, or an assumption you clearly label as one.

✗ Needs Work ★

  • You never reach a payback number.
  • Numbers show up with no source.
  • Vendor marketing is treated as fact.
  • The labor savings is never measured, or it isn't connected to the model.

Lane depth: your lane's numbers are sourced or clearly labeled as assumptions.

Criterion 2 of 3

THD Fit & Specificity

30% of your score

Does your model fit The Home Depot (THD) specifically, with its forklifts, high shelves, and heavy damage?

✓ Exceeds ★★★★★

  • You base your damage rate on Home Depot's own data (about 5–8% a year store-wide), then test it at 5%, 10%, 15%, and 25% for rough areas like lumber and outdoor garden.
  • You include the cost of replacing damaged labels.
  • You say what it would take to actually test ESL on specific products or aisles at Home Depot.

✗ Needs Work ★

  • It reads like a generic store, not Home Depot.
  • You ignore forklift zones, locked cages, and the past failure: Home Depot told us its whole-store ESL test about 4–5 years ago failed because labels kept getting damaged.
  • You use average damage rates with no warehouse adjustment.
  • You don't say what it would take to put ESL in place.

Lane depth: your lane ties to MET's real stores and data (the Partner Data Card, Store A and Store B).

Criterion 3 of 3

AI Integration + Human Judgment

30% of your score

Do you use AI to speed up the work while keeping the thinking your own?

✓ Exceeds ★★★★★

  • You use AI (like Claude) to work faster: setting up assumptions, checking math, testing scenarios.
  • You show where you checked, fixed, or overruled it against a real source.
  • The thinking is clearly yours.

✗ Needs Work ★

  • You paste AI output in as-is.
  • Made-up specs, rates, or costs are shown as fact.
  • There are no notes on what you checked.
  • Nothing shows you reviewed or corrected the AI.

Lane depth: you checked the AI on your lane's inputs, not just the shared model.
