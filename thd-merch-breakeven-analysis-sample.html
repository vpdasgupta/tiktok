Step 1 — Current-State Labor Cost

ILLUSTRATIVE — not THD data

The business case starts with the money ESL could save: the yearly cost of manually re-labeling this category by hand.

Formula: Price changes per year × seconds per change × fully-loaded labor rate = annual labor cost

Step 1 calculation chain for the seasonal endcap example. ILLUSTRATIVE — not THD data.
Line Formula Value
Labels in category — 500
Price changes / label / year — 48 (roughly monthly, plus seasonal & promo resets)
Seconds / manual change — 5 sec (MET's rate)
Fully-loaded labor rate — $25/hour (≈ $0.0069/sec)
Total manual changes / year 500 × 48 24,000 changes
Total labor time 24,000 × 5 sec 120,000 sec ≈ 33.3 hrs/year
Annual labor cost 33.3 hrs × $25/hr ≈ $833/year
Read it this way: $833 is the annual prize for this one small category — the recurring cost ESL would remove. It looks modest on purpose: ESL only pays off when savings, stacked up over time, finally clear the upfront and replacement costs below.

Step 2 — The ESL Cost Stack

ILLUSTRATIVE — not THD data

ESL isn't one price — it's a stack of costs. In a warehouse, the replacement line is the one traditional retail cases underestimate.

ESL cost stack for the 500-label seasonal endcap category. ILLUSTRATIVE — not THD data.
Cost line Assumption Amount
Hardware (ESL units) 500 labels × $8 each $4,000 (one-time)
Installation 500 labels × $2 labor to mount $1,000 (one-time)
Infrastructure (gateways / access points) Shared network hardware, allocated $800 (one-time)
Software (platform / license) Annual license, allocated to this category $300/year
Replacement (damage) 500 × damage rate × $8 (see sensitivity, Step 3) Varies with damage rate
One-time upfront ≈ $5,800 (hardware $4,000 + installation $1,000 + infrastructure $800), plus $300/year software, plus a replacement cost that depends entirely on the damage rate.
Watch out: Traditional retail ESL cases assume only 2–5% of labels get damaged per year. In a Home Depot aisle with forklifts, ladders, and pallet strikes, that number could be far higher — modeling only the optimistic rate is exactly the mistake this program exists to catch.

Step 3 — Breakeven & Sensitivity

ILLUSTRATIVE — not THD data

Breakeven is the month cumulative labor savings ($833/year, ≈ $69/month) finally equal cumulative ESL cost ($5,800 upfront + $300/year software + replacement). Because replacement scales with damage rate, breakeven moves with it — tested here at all four required rates.

Breakeven sensitivity across four damage-rate assumptions. ILLUSTRATIVE — not THD data.
Annual damage rate Annual replacement cost
(500 × rate × $8)		 Total annual cost
(replacement + $300 software)		 Approx. months to breakeven
5% (traditional-retail assumption) $200 $500 ≈ 10 months
10% (moderate warehouse) $400 $700 ≈ 14 months
15% (high-traffic bay) $600 $900 ≈ 20 months
25% (forklift-heavy zone) $1,000 $1,300 ≈ 30+ months
Read top to bottom: at a traditional-retail 5% damage rate, this category pays back in under a year. Push damage to 25% and payback stretches past two and a half years — every extra month is more replacement cost eating the labor savings. The technology didn't change between rows; only the damage rate did.

Step 4 — Favorable vs. Unfavorable

ILLUSTRATIVE — not THD data

Not every product pays off. Contrast the favorable seasonal endcap above with an unfavorable, slow-moving stable product.

Factor Favorable: seasonal endcap Unfavorable: stable staple product

Why it matters: for the stable product, ESL costs more to own and maintain than it ever saves in labor — the savings can't keep up with the yearly replacement and software bill, so the lines never cross. Same technology, same store, opposite conclusion. The difference is price-change frequency, and spotting that pattern is the real deliverable: a selective recommendation, not a store-wide one.

The 2-Page Summary, Filled In

Your model produces a number; your 2-page summary explains what it means. The required five-part skeleton, filled with the illustrative example.

ILLUSTRATIVE — not THD data

1 · Executive overview

Under realistic warehouse assumptions (10% damage), a high-price-change seasonal endcap category breaks even on ESL in roughly 14 months. The case is sensitive to damage: at 25% damage, payback stretches beyond 30 months. ESL is promising for high-frequency categories, but not universally.

2 · Key findings by category

High-price-change categories (seasonal endcaps, promotional zones) reach payback within ~1–2 years. Low-price-change staples never reach payback — their labor savings are too small to clear replacement and software costs. Payback speed tracks price-change frequency more than anything else.

3 · Critical assumptions

Fully-loaded labor rate ($25/hr), seconds per change (5), hardware cost ($8/label), and — most uncertain — the annual damage rate. Damage rate is where uncertainty is highest and where HD-provided data matters most.

4 · Damage-rate impact

Moving damage from 5% to 25% roughly triples the time to breakeven for the favorable category (≈10 → ≈30+ months). Damage is the single assumption most capable of turning a "deploy" into a "don't."

5 · Preliminary deployment criteria

A product looks like a good ESL candidate when it has high price-change frequency, sits in a lower-damage location, and shows a breakeven under ~24 months even at a stress-tested damage rate.

Reminder: every figure in that summary is illustrative. A submitted summary must rest on Home Depot-provided data or clearly-cited benchmarks — and the critical-assumptions section is where sources and honesty about uncertainty are shown.
